"Ozempic face" is a popular term for the hollow, sagging or older-looking facial appearance that some people notice after losing a lot of weight on GLP-1 medicines such as Ozempic or Wegovy (semaglutide) and Mounjaro (tirzepatide). It is not a medical diagnosis, and it is not listed as a side effect in the UK product information. Facial changes and loose skin are generally understood to be consequences of substantial weight loss itself, which reduces fat in the face and body faster than skin can adapt. Age, genetics, sun damage and smoking strongly influence how noticeable they are. This guide explains what is happening, what may help and how to approach cosmetic options safely.

<KeyTakeaways>
- "Ozempic face" is a media term, not a clinical condition. Similar facial changes can occur after major weight loss from diet, exercise or surgery.
- Facial fat helps give the face volume. When it decreases, the face can look thinner, more hollow or more lined.
- Loose skin is more likely with larger and faster weight loss, older age, genetics, sun damage and smoking.
- Eating enough protein, strength training and steady rather than extreme weight loss may help body composition, but there is little direct evidence they prevent loose skin.
- Surgery is the only way to remove significant excess skin. It is usually considered cosmetic and not routinely available on the NHS.
- If concerns about appearance are affecting your wellbeing, talk to your GP. Weight-loss treatment decisions belong to you and your prescriber.
</KeyTakeaways>

## What people mean by "Ozempic face"

The phrase became widespread on social media and in the press as GLP-1 medicines became more widely used. People typically describe:

- Hollowing of the cheeks and temples
- More prominent lines and folds, such as from the nose to the corners of the mouth
- Sagging around the jawline or neck
- Looking tired or older than before

The same changes have long been recognised after significant weight loss by other means, including bariatric surgery. What is different about GLP-1 medicines is the scale: very large numbers of people are now losing substantial amounts of weight, sometimes relatively quickly, and the effects are more visible and talked about.

<Callout type="evidence">
There is currently no high-quality evidence that GLP-1 medicines affect facial skin or fat directly, independent of weight loss. Research on facial changes specifically in people taking these medicines is limited. The explanations below reflect general understanding of weight loss and skin ageing.
</Callout>

## Why weight loss changes the face

### Facial fat and volume

The face contains several distinct fat compartments that give it fullness and contour, particularly around the cheeks, temples and under the eyes. These naturally reduce and shift downward with age. When you lose a lot of weight, fat is lost from the face as well as the body, and the face can look leaner or more hollow.

### Skin elasticity

Skin is supported by collagen and elastin, proteins that give it strength and allow it to spring back. With age, sun exposure and smoking, these fibres break down and the skin becomes less elastic. If skin has been stretched over a larger volume for many years, it may not fully retract when that volume is lost.

### The speed of weight loss

Many people believe faster weight loss leads to more sagging because skin has less time to adapt. This is plausible and widely reported, but robust studies comparing fast and slow weight loss on facial appearance are lacking.

### Age and genetics

Younger people's skin usually retracts better. Genetics influence skin quality, facial structure and where your body stores fat, which is why two people losing the same amount of weight can look very different afterwards.

## Loose skin on the body

Loose or excess skin is common after major weight loss, especially on the:

- Abdomen
- Upper arms
- Thighs
- Breasts or chest
- Neck
- Buttocks

For most people this is mainly a cosmetic concern, but for some it can cause practical problems. These can include chafing, rashes or skin infections in folds, difficulty finding clothes that fit, discomfort during exercise, and effects on confidence and intimacy.

### What influences loose skin

| Factor | Effect |
|---|---|
| Amount of weight lost | Larger losses are more likely to leave excess skin |
| Duration of excess weight | Skin stretched for many years may retract less |
| Age | Skin tends to be less elastic with age |
| Genetics | Influences skin quality and fat distribution |
| Sun exposure | UV damage breaks down collagen and elastin |
| Smoking | Damages skin and impairs healing |
| Pregnancy history | Previous stretching of abdominal skin |
| Speed of weight loss | Often thought to matter, though evidence is limited |

## Muscle, body composition and appearance

When anyone loses weight, some of what is lost is lean tissue, including muscle, rather than only fat. Body composition sub-studies within major trials of semaglutide and tirzepatide found that, while most weight lost was fat, a meaningful proportion was lean mass. This is broadly similar to what is seen with other weight-loss methods, but it matters for appearance as well as strength.

Muscle provides shape and support under the skin. Preserving or building muscle may improve how areas such as the arms, thighs and buttocks look, even if it cannot tighten skin itself. It also supports metabolic health, mobility and long-term weight maintenance.

## What may help: steps you can take

There is no proven way to prevent facial changes or loose skin. These steps are based on general principles of healthy weight loss and skin care.

### 1. Follow your prescribed plan

GLP-1 doses are increased gradually under a prescriber's supervision. Do not increase faster or take more than prescribed in the hope of quicker results. Unregulated or excessive use increases the risk of side effects without proper oversight. If you feel you are losing weight much faster than expected, discuss it with your prescriber.

### 2. Eat enough protein

Protein supports muscle maintenance and is a building block for skin proteins such as collagen. Because GLP-1 medicines reduce appetite, many people struggle to eat enough. Our guide to [protein on GLP-1 medication](/guides/protein-on-glp1) suggests practical ways to prioritise it.

### 3. Strength training

The NHS physical activity guidelines recommend that adults do strengthening activities that work all the major muscle groups on at least two days a week, alongside at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity. Resistance exercise is particularly important during weight loss to help preserve muscle.

### 4. Protect your skin

- Use broad-spectrum sunscreen and limit sun exposure, as UV damage accelerates skin ageing.
- Avoid smoking, which damages collagen and slows healing.
- Moisturise to help keep skin comfortable, although moisturisers cannot tighten loose skin.

### 5. Stay hydrated and eat a balanced diet

Good hydration and a varied diet with fruit, vegetables, healthy fats and wholegrains support overall health, including skin. See our guides to [what to eat on GLP-1 medication](/guides/what-to-eat-on-glp1) and [vitamins and supplements on GLP-1 medication](/guides/vitamins-and-supplements-on-glp1).

### 6. Be cautious about skin products and supplements

Many creams, collagen supplements and devices are marketed to tighten skin. Evidence that they make a meaningful difference to loose skin after major weight loss is weak. Be wary of products making dramatic claims.

### 7. Give it time

Skin may continue to adapt for months after weight stabilises. Many surgeons advise waiting until weight has been stable for some time before considering skin removal surgery.

## Hair and other appearance changes

Facial changes and loose skin are not the only visible effects. Hair loss is listed as a common side effect of both Mounjaro and Wegovy in their UK SmPCs, particularly in the weight-management trials. In pooled SURMOUNT weight-management trials, hair loss was reported in 4.9% of people on tirzepatide compared with 1.0% on placebo. In Wegovy's phase 3 trials, it was reported in 2.5% on semaglutide 2.4 mg compared with 1.0% on placebo. The Wegovy SmPC notes it was reported more often in people with greater weight loss and that most people recovered while continuing treatment. Read more in our guide to [Mounjaro and hair loss](/guides/mounjaro-hair-loss).

## Caring for skin folds day to day

If you have loose skin, especially on the abdomen, under the breasts, in the groin or on the thighs, some simple measures can reduce discomfort and the risk of skin problems:

- **Keep folds clean and dry.** Wash gently with water or a mild, unperfumed wash and pat dry thoroughly, particularly after exercise or in hot weather.
- **Reduce friction.** Breathable, moisture-wicking fabrics and well-fitting underwear or support garments can help. Some people find compression garments useful during exercise.
- **Watch for rashes.** Red, sore, moist or itchy skin in folds can be intertrigo, sometimes complicated by fungal or bacterial infection. A pharmacist can advise on suitable creams, and your GP can assess persistent or recurrent problems.
- **Do not ignore infection.** Spreading redness, heat, swelling, pain or a high temperature need prompt medical advice.

Recording any recurrent skin problems, with dates and treatments tried, can also be useful if you later ask your GP about whether a referral for surgery might be considered.

## Common myths

| Myth | What the evidence suggests |
|---|---|
| "GLP-1 medicines melt your face" | There is no evidence of a direct effect on facial tissue; changes reflect fat loss and skin elasticity |
| "Only people on GLP-1s get this" | Similar changes are well recognised after major weight loss by any method, including surgery |
| "A special cream will tighten loose skin" | Topical products have little proven effect on significant loose skin |
| "Collagen supplements prevent sagging" | Evidence in this setting is weak and inconsistent |
| "Exercise can remove loose skin" | Exercise builds muscle and may improve shape, but cannot remove excess skin |
| "Losing weight slowly always prevents loose skin" | Slower loss may help some people, but age, genetics and the amount lost matter a great deal |

## Talking to your prescriber about the pace of weight loss

If you are worried about facial changes or loose skin, it is reasonable to raise it at your review. Questions you might ask include:

- Is my current rate of weight loss within the range you would expect?
- Do I need to continue increasing my dose, or could I stay at my current dose?
- Am I eating enough, particularly enough protein, to support muscle?
- Could I be referred for dietetic or physical activity support?
- What would a sensible target or maintenance plan look like for me?

Not everyone needs to reach the highest dose, and prescribers can tailor the plan. Any change to your dose should be agreed with your prescriber rather than made on your own. Our guide to [long-term GLP-1 use](/guides/long-term-glp1-use) discusses maintenance in more detail.

## Cosmetic and surgical options

Some people choose cosmetic treatments after weight loss. These are personal decisions with real risks, and it is wise to wait until your weight is stable, as your face and body may keep changing.

### Non-surgical facial treatments

Options include dermal fillers, skin-tightening devices and other aesthetic treatments. Key points:

- **Risks are real.** Fillers can cause bruising, swelling, lumps, infection and allergic reactions. Rarely, filler injected into a blood vessel can block blood flow, causing skin damage or, very rarely, vision loss.
- **Choose practitioners carefully.** The NHS advises researching who will carry out any cosmetic procedure, checking their qualifications and registration, and avoiding being rushed. The NHS signposts organisations including Save Face, the British Association of Medical Aesthetic Nurses and the British College of Aesthetic Medicine.
- **Regulation is changing.** The UK government has been developing a licensing scheme for non-surgical cosmetic procedures in England. Check current rules before booking.

### Surgical skin removal

Body-contouring operations include tummy tuck (abdominoplasty), arm lift, thigh lift, breast surgery and facelift. According to the NHS:

- A tummy tuck can remove excess loose skin and fat and tighten the abdominal muscles. It aims to remove skin that cannot be removed through exercise, for example after pregnancy or losing a lot of weight.
- It is usually only recommended for people with a healthy BMI and is not a weight-loss treatment.
- It is regarded as cosmetic surgery, so it is not usually available on the NHS.
- It is major surgery with risks, and the NHS advises taking time to reflect on the decision.
- In England, independent clinics and hospitals providing cosmetic surgery must be registered with the Care Quality Commission, and surgeons must be registered with the General Medical Council.

In some cases, where excess skin causes significant functional problems such as recurrent infections that do not respond to other treatment, NHS funding may be considered under local policies. Criteria vary by area, so ask your GP.

### Surgery while taking a GLP-1

If you are planning any surgery under general anaesthetic or deep sedation while taking a GLP-1 medicine, tell your surgeon and anaesthetist. These medicines slow stomach emptying, and the SmPCs advise that the increased risk of residual stomach contents should be considered before such procedures. Our guide to [GLP-1 medication, surgery and anaesthesia](/guides/glp1-surgery-and-anaesthesia) explains more.

## Body image and wellbeing

Changing appearance can bring mixed emotions. Many people feel more confident after weight loss, but some are disappointed by loose skin or facial changes, or find unsolicited comments difficult.

Signs that appearance concerns may be affecting your mental health include:

- Spending a lot of time worrying about a specific area of your appearance
- Constantly checking mirrors or avoiding them entirely
- Avoiding social situations because of how you look
- Feeling that cosmetic treatments are the only way to feel okay

The NHS describes body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) as a mental health condition where a person spends a lot of time worrying about flaws in their appearance, often unnoticeable to others. If this sounds familiar, speak to your GP. Our guide to [GLP-1 medication and mental health](/guides/glp1-and-mental-health) covers wider wellbeing on treatment.

## Keeping perspective on health benefits

For many people, the health benefits of losing excess weight, such as better blood sugar control, lower blood pressure, improved mobility and reduced risk of weight-related conditions, outweigh cosmetic concerns. But how you feel about your body matters too, and it is reasonable to raise it with your prescriber. They can help you consider whether your current rate of weight loss and your overall plan are right for you.

Our guides to [realistic weight-loss expectations](/guides/realistic-weight-loss-expectations) and [measuring progress beyond the scales](/guides/measuring-progress-beyond-the-scales) may help you think about success in broader terms.

## Reporting side effects

If you experience any suspected side effect from a GLP-1 medicine, including hair loss or unexpected skin changes, you can report it via the MHRA Yellow Card scheme, online or using the app. Reports help the MHRA monitor the safety of medicines in real-world use.

## When to speak to a professional

**Speak to your GP or prescriber if:**

- You are losing weight much faster than expected
- Skin folds are causing rashes, infections or pain
- Concerns about your appearance are affecting your mood, relationships or daily life
- You are considering cosmetic surgery and want to understand NHS criteria or risks
- You notice significant hair loss

**Seek urgent medical advice (NHS 111 or your GP) if:**

- You have a red, hot, swollen or painful area of skin, especially with a high temperature, which could be an infection
- You develop pain, blanching, dusky discolouration or vision changes after a filler treatment. Contact the practitioner and seek urgent medical help immediately.
- You have signs of dehydration, such as dark urine, peeing less often or dizziness

**Call 999 or go to A&E if:**

- You have severe, persistent abdominal pain, especially if it spreads to your back, with or without vomiting, which may be a sign of pancreatitis
- You notice yellowing of the skin or the whites of your eyes (jaundice) with pain or fever
- You have sudden loss of vision
- You have signs of a severe allergic reaction, such as swelling of the face or throat or difficulty breathing

If you are struggling with your mental health or having thoughts of suicide, call NHS 111 (option 2 for mental health in many areas), contact Samaritans free on 116 123, or call 999 in an emergency.
