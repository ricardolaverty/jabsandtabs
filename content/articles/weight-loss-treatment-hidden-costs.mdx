The cost of private weight loss injections such as Mounjaro or Wegovy is more than the monthly price of the pen. Depending on the provider, you may also pay for consultations, needles, sharps bins and delivery, and the price usually rises as your dose increases. Beyond the provider, there can be spending on side-effect remedies, contraception, supplements, clothing, exercise and possibly blood tests, plus the longer-term cost of staying on treatment. This guide sets out the costs people often overlook, so you can plan realistically. We do not quote prices; see our [prices page](/prices) for current comparisons of regulated providers.

<KeyTakeaways>
- The advertised monthly price may not include consultations, reviews, needles, sharps bins or delivery. Always check what is included.
- Many providers charge more for higher doses, so costs often rise during the first few months of treatment.
- Out-of-pocket extras can include laxatives, rehydration salts or antacids for side effects, additional contraception, supplements and new clothes.
- Some GP practices charge for private letters or forms that online providers may request.
- Weight regain after stopping is common (STEP 1 extension, SURMOUNT-4), so the real cost depends on how long you stay on treatment.
- Cost should never drive dosing decisions or push anyone towards unregulated sellers. Speak to your prescriber if cost is a concern.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Why the headline price is only part of the picture

Private weight loss treatment in the UK is usually bought as a package from an online pharmacy or clinic. The package combines the medicine, the prescriber's assessment, ongoing clinical oversight and delivery. Providers present this in different ways: some quote a single all-in monthly figure, others separate the medicine from fees, and others advertise a low starting price that rises with the dose.

None of these models is wrong, but they make like-for-like comparisons difficult. Our guide to [how weight loss injection pricing works](/guides/how-weight-loss-injection-pricing-works) explains the building blocks. This article focuses on what tends to get missed.

For background on the medicines themselves, see the hub on [weight loss injections](/weight-loss-injections) and our comparison of [every UK weight loss injection](/guides/weight-loss-injections-compared).

## Costs from your provider

### Consultation and review fees

Weight loss injections are prescription-only medicines, so a registered prescriber must assess you before the first supply and review you at intervals. Some providers include all consultations in the medicine price; others charge an initial consultation fee, a fee for each review or a fee for changing your dose.

General Pharmaceutical Council (GPhC) guidance, strengthened in 2025, expects online pharmacies to verify the information given for weight loss medicines rather than rely solely on an online questionnaire. Verification might involve a video call, photo or video evidence of your weight, or contacting your GP. These checks are an important safety measure. Whether they carry a separate charge varies by provider.

### Dose escalation

Each strength of Mounjaro comes in a separate pen. Most people start at 2.5mg and increase every four weeks or more, depending on response and side effects. Since Eli Lilly's September 2025 UK list-price change for private supply, higher strengths generally carry higher list prices, and many providers charge more as the dose goes up. See our article on the [2025 Mounjaro price rise](/guides/mounjaro-price-rise-2025).

This means the cost in month four or five can be noticeably higher than in month one. Wegovy also steps up over several months to a 2.4mg maintenance dose, and providers may price strengths differently.

| Treatment phase | What typically happens | Cost implication |
|---|---|---|
| Starting dose | Low starter dose for about four weeks | Often the lowest-priced month |
| Titration | Dose increased in steps, usually every four weeks | Price may rise at each step |
| Maintenance | Settled on an effective, tolerated dose | Ongoing monthly cost at that strength |
| Holding or reducing | Dose held or lowered because of side effects | Cost may stay flat or fall |
| Pausing or stopping | Treatment interrupted | Possible unused pens; restarting may mean going back to a lower dose |

### Needles, sharps bins and swabs

Most injectable pens need a fresh needle for each injection. Whether needles come in the medicine pack depends on the product, so check the patient information leaflet. If they are not included, your provider may supply them free, sell them separately, or expect you to source them.

Used needles must go in a sharps bin. Some providers include one with the first order; others charge. Disposal arrangements for full sharps bins vary by local council and pharmacy, and a few councils charge for collection.

### Delivery

GLP-1 pens need to be refrigerated before first use, so they are usually sent in insulated packaging by tracked courier. Delivery may be free, included in the price, or charged per order, with extra charges for named-day or weekend delivery. If you are not in when the parcel arrives, re-delivery may cost extra, and a delayed parcel could affect whether the medicine stayed within its temperature range. Our guide to [Mounjaro storage and travel](/guides/mounjaro-storage-and-travel) explains storage requirements.

### Subscriptions and cancellation terms

Some providers offer subscriptions. These can be convenient, but check:

- whether there is a minimum term
- how much notice you need to give to pause or cancel
- what happens if your prescriber advises a dose change or a pause
- whether a clinical check still happens before each supply

A subscription should never mean supply continues without appropriate clinical review.

## Costs outside your provider

### Managing side effects

The most common side effects of GLP-1 medicines are nausea, diarrhoea, constipation, vomiting, indigestion and reflux. They are usually worst when starting or increasing the dose. Many people manage with simple changes, such as eating smaller meals, eating slowly, avoiding rich or fatty food and drinking enough fluids. Some also use over-the-counter products, for example:

- laxatives or fibre supplements for constipation
- oral rehydration salts after diarrhoea or vomiting
- antacids or reflux treatments for indigestion

Ask a pharmacist before using any remedy, as some products are not suitable alongside certain conditions or medicines. Persistent or severe symptoms should be discussed with your prescriber rather than managed alone. See our guides to [nausea on GLP-1s](/guides/nausea-on-glp1) and [constipation on GLP-1s](/guides/constipation-on-glp1).

### Contraception

The MHRA has advised that tirzepatide (Mounjaro) may reduce the effectiveness of oral contraceptives in people who are overweight or obese. It recommends switching to a non-oral method, or adding a barrier method such as condoms, for four weeks after starting Mounjaro and for four weeks after each dose increase. The MHRA has also reminded people that GLP-1 medicines should not be used in pregnancy and should be stopped before a planned pregnancy.

Contraception is free on the NHS through GP practices and sexual health services, but some people pay for condoms or other methods privately for convenience. See [GLP-1s and contraception](/guides/glp1-and-contraception) and [GLP-1s, pregnancy and fertility](/guides/glp1-pregnancy-and-fertility).

### Food and nutrition

Many people find their food spending falls because they eat less. However, prescribers and dietitians generally recommend prioritising protein, vegetables, fruit, wholegrains and fluids to protect muscle and limit side effects, and some of these foods cost more than the convenience foods they replace. Some people also use protein shakes or high-protein snacks.

Whether your food bill goes up or down overall is individual and should not be relied on to offset treatment costs. Our guides to [what to eat on a GLP-1](/guides/what-to-eat-on-glp1) and [protein on a GLP-1](/guides/protein-on-glp1) offer practical, budget-conscious advice.

### Supplements

Eating less can make it harder to get enough of some nutrients. There is no blanket recommendation that everyone on a GLP-1 needs supplements, and NHS advice on vitamin D in autumn and winter applies to the whole population. If your diet is very limited, a prescriber, pharmacist or dietitian can advise whether anything is worth taking. See [vitamins and supplements on GLP-1s](/guides/vitamins-and-supplements-on-glp1).

### Exercise and strength training

Physical activity is part of the licensed use of all weight loss injections, which are prescribed alongside a reduced-calorie diet and increased activity. Activity does not have to cost money: walking, bodyweight exercises at home and free council or NHS resources all count. Some people choose gym memberships, classes or personal trainers, particularly for strength training to help preserve muscle. See [exercise on GLP-1s](/guides/exercise-on-glp1) and [preserving muscle on GLP-1s](/guides/preserving-muscle-on-glp1).

### Clothing

Losing a substantial amount of weight often means replacing clothes, sometimes more than once as sizes change. This is easy to forget when budgeting. Some people use second-hand shops, clothes swaps or alterations to manage the cost during the months when their size is changing most.

### Blood tests and health checks

Your prescriber may want recent blood test results, for example kidney function, blood sugar (HbA1c) or cholesterol, depending on your health. Your GP may already have these on record. If you choose private blood tests, they come at extra cost. People with type 2 diabetes need closer monitoring, because medicines such as insulin or sulfonylureas may need adjusting to avoid low blood sugar. Your GP should be informed that you are taking a weight loss medicine.

### GP fees for private paperwork

Some online providers ask for information from your GP, such as confirmation of your medical history. Providing information for private services is not always part of NHS-funded GP work, and GP practices can charge for some private letters, reports or forms. Policies vary, so ask your practice. Viewing and sharing your own records through the NHS App may be an alternative in some cases.

### Travel

If you travel while on treatment, you may need a cool bag, a letter from your prescriber for airport security, and travel insurance that covers your medical conditions and medicines. You should declare relevant conditions and medicines to your insurer, which might affect your premium.

### Loose skin

Significant weight loss can leave excess skin, particularly on the abdomen, arms and thighs. NHS funding for body-contouring surgery is very limited and usually restricted to people meeting strict criteria. Private cosmetic surgery is expensive and carries its own risks. Many people do not need or want surgery, but it is worth being aware of. See our guide to [facial changes and loose skin](/guides/facial-changes-and-loose-skin).

## Non-financial costs worth considering

Not every cost is measured in money. Before starting, it can help to think about the time, energy and emotional effort that treatment can involve.

### Time

Private treatment involves an initial assessment, verification checks, regular reviews, being available for deliveries and, for weekly injections, setting aside a few minutes each week to inject. Daily injections, such as Saxenda, need a daily routine. Most of this is manageable, but it adds up over a year, especially if you also commit to regular exercise, meal planning and health appointments.

### Days affected by side effects

For some people, nausea, tiredness or digestive symptoms are mild and short-lived. For others, particularly in the days after a dose increase, they can affect work, sleep or social plans. In clinical trials such as SURMOUNT-1 and STEP 1, gastrointestinal side effects were the most common reason people stopped treatment, although most participants continued. Some people plan dose increases around a quieter week. If side effects are significant, your prescriber may suggest staying on a dose for longer before increasing, which can also mean staying on a lower-priced strength for longer.

### Social and emotional adjustment

Eating less can change social routines built around meals and drinks. Some people also find it difficult to deal with comments about their weight loss or their decision to use medication. Alcohol tolerance may change, and many people choose to drink less. Our guides to [Mounjaro and alcohol](/guides/mounjaro-and-alcohol) and [GLP-1s and mental health](/guides/glp1-and-mental-health) look at these aspects.

## Questions to ask a provider before you start

Asking these questions before your first order can help avoid surprises later:

- Is there a consultation fee, and is it charged once or at every review?
- Does the price change as my dose increases, and what is the price at each strength?
- Are needles and a sharps bin included? If not, how do I get them?
- What does delivery cost, and what happens if a parcel is delayed or I miss it?
- If I am on a subscription, how do I pause or cancel, and is there a minimum term?
- How quickly can I reach a clinician if I have side effects?
- Will you contact my GP, and will you need anything from my GP practice?
- What happens to my plan if I need to stop for surgery, illness or pregnancy?
- If the manufacturer's list price changes, how and when will I be told?

A clear, unhurried answer to each of these is a good sign. Pressure to commit quickly, or vagueness about fees, is not.

## The biggest hidden cost: time on treatment

The most important factor in the total cost is how long you stay on treatment. Clinical trials show that weight is often regained after GLP-1 medicines are stopped:

| Trial | What happened after stopping or switching to placebo |
|---|---|
| STEP 1 extension (semaglutide) | Around two-thirds of the weight lost was regained within a year of stopping |
| SURMOUNT-4 (tirzepatide) | People switched to placebo after 36 weeks regained a substantial proportion of the weight lost, while those who continued lost more |

Because obesity is increasingly treated as a chronic condition, many people stay on medication for long periods, which means costs continue. NICE TA875 limits NHS Wegovy treatment within specialist services to a maximum of two years, but private treatment has no fixed limit. Our guide to [the long-term cost of GLP-1 treatment](/guides/long-term-cost-of-glp1-treatment) explores how to plan for this, and [weight regain after stopping](/guides/weight-regain-after-stopping-glp1) covers what the evidence shows.

### Switching and restarting

Changing provider, switching between medicines or restarting after a break can also add costs:

- a new consultation and verification checks with a new provider
- a possible gap in supply while the switch is arranged
- restarting at a lower dose if too long has passed since your last injection, which means repeating part of the titration
- leftover pens at a dose you no longer need

Our guides to [switching providers mid-treatment](/guides/switching-providers-mid-treatment) and [switching from Wegovy to Mounjaro](/guides/switching-wegovy-to-mounjaro) explain how to do this safely.

## Comparing the full cost

When comparing providers, try to estimate the cost over six to twelve months, including:

1. the price of each strength you might reasonably use
2. consultation and review fees
3. needles, sharps bins and delivery
4. any subscription or cancellation terms
5. the level of clinical support included

Then add a realistic allowance for personal costs outside the provider, such as side-effect remedies, clothing and any blood tests. Our [providers page](/providers) and [prices page](/prices) compare regulated UK services on what they include.

## Checking whether the NHS is an option

Before committing to private treatment, it is worth asking your GP whether you might be eligible for NHS care. NICE TA1026 recommends Mounjaro for adults with a BMI of at least 35 and at least one weight-related condition, with NHS England phasing access from June 2025, starting with people with the highest BMIs and multiple conditions. NICE TA875 recommends Wegovy within specialist weight management services. Eligibility is limited and waits can be long, but NHS treatment costs no more than the standard prescription charge in England, and prescriptions are free in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland. See [private vs NHS weight loss treatment](/guides/private-vs-nhs-weight-loss-treatment).

## Cost and safety

When treatment becomes expensive, it can be tempting to cut corners. Please avoid:

- obtaining pens from social media, messaging apps or unregistered websites, where products may be fake or contaminated
- drawing extra doses from pens or sharing pens
- skipping doses or stretching the interval between injections without advice
- using a diabetes-licensed product such as Ozempic without a prescriber's agreement

If cost is a problem, tell your prescriber. They may be able to discuss options, such as staying at a lower effective dose if clinically appropriate, a different medicine, or a planned approach to stopping. Our guide to [fake weight loss jabs](/guides/fake-weight-loss-jabs) explains how to spot unsafe sellers.

## When to speak to a professional

Speak to your GP, pharmacist or prescriber before starting treatment, before changing your dose and before stopping. Let your GP know you are taking a weight loss medicine, especially if you have diabetes or take other regular medicines.

Seek urgent medical help (NHS 111, or 999 in an emergency) if you have:

- severe, persistent abdominal pain, possibly spreading to your back, with or without vomiting (possible pancreatitis)
- pain in the upper right abdomen, fever, or yellowing of the skin or eyes (possible gallbladder problems)
- persistent vomiting or diarrhoea with signs of dehydration, such as dizziness or passing very little urine
- signs of a severe allergic reaction, such as swelling of the face or throat or difficulty breathing
- symptoms of low blood sugar if you also take diabetes medicines

Report suspected side effects to the MHRA through the Yellow Card scheme.
