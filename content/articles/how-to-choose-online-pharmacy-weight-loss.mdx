To choose an online pharmacy for weight loss treatment in the UK, start by confirming it is registered with the General Pharmaceutical Council (GPhC), then look at how carefully it assesses you, how it verifies your weight and identity, what clinical support it offers during treatment, and how transparent it is about costs. Good services take a proper medical history, involve a named prescriber, and are prepared to say no. This guide gives you a step-by-step checklist. We don't rank or recommend individual pharmacies here; instead, we explain what to look for so you can make an informed choice with your prescriber. For related reading, see our [provider guides](/guides/topic/providers) and the full library of [GLP-1 guides](/guides).

<KeyTakeaways>
- Every pharmacy in Great Britain, including online pharmacies, must be registered with the GPhC. In Northern Ireland, pharmacies register with the Pharmaceutical Society of Northern Ireland (PSNI).
- Check the register yourself on the regulator's website, rather than relying on a logo or link on the pharmacy's own site.
- A safe service carries out a real clinical assessment, verifies your identity and weight independently, and may contact your GP.
- Look for named, registered prescribers and pharmacists, clear aftercare, and an easy way to speak to a clinician.
- Compare total costs over time, not just the starting price, and understand what is included.
- Be wary of urgency, discount pressure, "no consultation needed" claims, or medicines sold outside the licensed range.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Why choosing carefully matters

Mounjaro (tirzepatide), Wegovy (semaglutide) and the newer GLP-1 tablets are prescription-only medicines. They are effective, but they also carry risks, including gastrointestinal side effects, dehydration, gallbladder problems and, rarely, pancreatitis. They should not be used in pregnancy, and they need careful thought in people with certain medical histories. Our guide to [who should not take GLP-1 medicines](/guides/who-should-not-take-glp1s) explains these cautions.

The quality of the service you use affects how well those risks are managed. A careful service checks you are suitable, starts at the right dose, reviews you before increasing it, and helps you if problems arise. A poor one may prescribe without proper checks, or may not be a legitimate pharmacy at all.

There is also the risk of fake products. The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) has repeatedly warned about unlicensed and counterfeit weight-loss injections sold online and through social media. Our guide to [fake and unlicensed weight loss jabs](/guides/fake-weight-loss-jabs) explains how to spot them.

## Step 1: Check registration

This is the most important step, and it only takes a few minutes.

### Find the pharmacy's details on its website

A legitimate online pharmacy should clearly display:

- The registered pharmacy's name and full address
- Its GPhC registration number (or PSNI number in Northern Ireland)
- The name of the superintendent pharmacist
- Details of the company that owns the pharmacy

These are often in the website footer or on an "About" or "Regulatory information" page.

### Search the GPhC register yourself

Go directly to the GPhC website (pharmacyregulation.org) rather than following a link from the pharmacy's own website. Search the register of pharmacies by name, postcode or registration number. Check that:

- The pharmacy appears on the register and its registration is current
- The address matches what the website says
- There are no conditions or concerns you would want to know about

You can also search the register of pharmacists to confirm the superintendent pharmacist, and any named pharmacist prescribers, are registered.

<Callout type="info">
Some registered pharmacies display a voluntary GPhC logo that links to their register entry. It can be helpful, but logos can be copied by illegitimate sites. Searching the register independently is more reliable.
</Callout>

### Check the prescriber

The pharmacy may employ its own prescribers, or it may work with a separate online doctor service. Either way, the website should make clear who prescribes. Prescribers should be registered with:

- The General Medical Council (GMC), for doctors
- The GPhC, for pharmacist independent prescribers
- The Nursing and Midwifery Council (NMC), for nurse independent prescribers

### Check the online doctor service, where relevant

If prescribing is carried out by a separate online doctor or clinical service, that service may need to be registered with a care regulator:

- England: Care Quality Commission (CQC)
- Wales: Healthcare Inspectorate Wales (HIW)
- Scotland: Healthcare Improvement Scotland (HIS)
- Northern Ireland: Regulation and Quality Improvement Authority (RQIA)

CQC inspection reports are published on its website and can give an insight into how a service operates. Our guide to [who regulates online weight loss services](/guides/who-regulates-online-weight-loss-services) explains how these regulators fit together.

## Step 2: Look at the clinical assessment

Once you know a pharmacy is registered, look at how it assesses people. You can often see this before you start, from the website's description of the process.

### Signs of a thorough assessment

- **A detailed medical questionnaire** covering BMI, weight-related conditions, medicines, allergies, pregnancy and contraception, and history of pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, eating disorders, thyroid cancer and mental health.
- **Identity checks**, such as an electronic ID check or photo ID.
- **Independent weight verification**, such as a video consultation, GP record check or pharmacy measurement. Since February 2025, GPhC guidance has said that prescribing weight-loss medicines should not be based on an online questionnaire alone.
- **GP information sharing**, with your consent.
- **A starting dose in line with the licence**, normally the lowest dose (2.5mg for Mounjaro, 0.25mg for Wegovy), unless you are continuing established treatment.
- **A clear process for declining** people who are not suitable, with an explanation and suggested next steps.

Our guide to [the online weight loss consultation](/guides/online-weight-loss-consultation) explains each of these in detail, and our guide to [why online pharmacies verify your ID and weight](/guides/id-and-weight-verification) covers verification.

### Warning signs

- You can pick a medicine and dose and pay before any assessment
- No questions about your health or medicines
- No attempt to verify weight or identity
- High starting doses offered without explanation
- Medicines offered outside their licence, or described as "research use", "compounded" or "generic tirzepatide"
- No named prescriber or pharmacy details

## Step 3: Understand the medicines on offer

A legitimate pharmacy supplies licensed medicines from the regulated supply chain. In the UK, at the time of writing, licensed GLP-1 options for weight management include:

| Medicine | Active ingredient | Form | UK licensing status (at the time of writing) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mounjaro | Tirzepatide | Weekly injection (KwikPen) | Licensed for weight management |
| Wegovy | Semaglutide | Weekly injection (FlexTouch) | Licensed; MHRA approved a maximum dose of up to 7.2mg in January 2026 for adults with obesity |
| Wegovy tablets | Semaglutide (oral) | Daily tablet | MHRA approved for weight management in June 2026 |
| Foundayo | Orforglipron | Daily tablet | MHRA approved in August 2026 |
| Saxenda and generic liraglutide | Liraglutide | Daily injection | Licensed for weight management |

Regulatory status can change. Check the current SmPC on medicines.org.uk or ask your pharmacist if you are unsure. Our guides to the [oral GLP-1 options](/oral-glp1) and [weight loss injections](/weight-loss-injections) explain each medicine.

A pharmacy that offers products not on this list, such as retatrutide (an investigational medicine that is not licensed in the UK at the time of writing), or "compounded semaglutide", is a serious red flag.

## Step 4: Assess ongoing clinical support

GLP-1 treatment is not a single transaction. It typically involves several months of dose increases and, for many people, long-term treatment. Look for:

- **Reviews before each dose increase**, including updated weight and side effects
- **Easy access to a pharmacist or prescriber** by message, phone or video, with clear response times
- **Practical guidance** on injecting, storing pens, missed doses and managing side effects such as nausea and constipation
- **Safety information** about red-flag symptoms and when to seek urgent care
- **Advice on diet, protein, hydration and physical activity**, or links to reputable resources
- **A plan for what happens if treatment isn't working**, or if you want to stop

NICE guidance for NHS treatment includes stopping rules: for example, NICE TA1026 advises considering stopping tirzepatide if less than 5% of initial weight is lost after six months on the highest tolerated dose. A responsible private service will also review whether treatment is still appropriate. See our guide to [GLP-1 non-responders](/guides/glp1-non-responders).

## Step 5: Check supply, packaging and delivery

GLP-1 pens need to be kept cool before first use. Mounjaro and Wegovy pens should be stored in a fridge before use, and the SmPCs allow limited periods out of the fridge below a stated temperature. Check the current patient information leaflet for details.

Look for a pharmacy that explains:

- How medicines are packaged to stay cool in transit
- What to do if a delivery is delayed or arrives warm
- Whether a signature is required
- How to dispose of used needles safely (some services provide sharps bins)

Our guide to [Mounjaro storage and travel](/guides/mounjaro-storage-and-travel) covers storage rules in more detail.

## Step 6: Compare costs transparently

Costs vary between pharmacies and over the course of treatment. We don't quote prices in our guides because they change often, and Lilly's increase to the UK list price of Mounjaro from September 2025 showed how quickly the market can shift. Instead, consider what affects the total you pay:

- **Dose:** Higher doses often cost more, so the starting price is rarely what you pay months later.
- **What's included:** Consultation, needles, sharps bin, delivery and ongoing reviews may be included or charged separately.
- **Subscriptions:** Some services use subscriptions. Check how to pause or cancel, and whether prices can change.
- **Consultation fees:** Some charge separately for assessments or reviews.
- **Price changes:** Ask how the pharmacy communicates price changes.

Our guides to [weight loss treatment hidden costs](/guides/weight-loss-treatment-hidden-costs) and [the long-term cost of GLP-1 treatment](/guides/long-term-cost-of-glp1-treatment) go further. You can compare current service costs on our [prices page](/prices).

Be cautious about heavy discounting, "first month free" offers or countdown timers. Prescription-only medicines must not be promoted to the public, and the Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) took action against several advertisers, including registered pharmacies, over weight-loss medicine adverts in 2025.

## Step 7: Look at transparency and complaints handling

A trustworthy pharmacy makes it easy to find:

- Its full regulatory information
- Its privacy notice, including how health data and ID documents are handled
- Terms and conditions in plain English
- A complaints procedure, and how to escalate a complaint to the GPhC or another regulator

Independent reviews can give an impression of customer service, but they are not a substitute for regulatory checks. Reviews say little about clinical safety.

## How to read an inspection report

Both the GPhC and the CQC publish inspection reports. They can tell you more than a registration entry alone.

### GPhC pharmacy inspections

GPhC inspection reports assess whether a pharmacy meets the standards for registered pharmacies. Reports are organised around principles that cover areas such as governance, staffing, premises, services and equipment. Each principle is assessed against the standards, and the report summarises whether they are met. When reading a report, look for:

- The overall outcome and the date of inspection
- Any standards that were not met, and what the pharmacy was asked to improve
- Comments about how the pharmacy manages online services and higher-risk medicines
- Whether there has been a more recent follow-up inspection

### CQC inspections

If an online doctor service is registered with the CQC, its reports describe how the service performs against the CQC's key questions, which include whether services are safe and well-led. Look at the date, any rating given, and any concerns identified.

An older report does not necessarily mean problems, but a recent report gives a more current picture. Inspection reports are one source of information among several, alongside how the service assesses and supports you.

## Special circumstances

Some people should take extra care when choosing a service, or may be better served by face-to-face care or close GP involvement.

### If you have type 2 diabetes

GLP-1 medicines can lower blood sugar. If you take insulin or a sulfonylurea, your diabetes medicines may need adjusting to avoid hypoglycaemia. Look for a service that asks detailed questions about your diabetes treatment and will work with your GP or diabetes team. See our guide to [GLP-1 medicines and type 2 diabetes](/guides/glp1-and-type-2-diabetes).

### If you are older

There is no upper age limit for GLP-1 medicines, but older adults may be more vulnerable to dehydration, muscle loss and the effects of eating much less. Look for a service that asks about frailty, other medicines and kidney function. See our guide to [GLP-1 medicines over 65](/guides/glp1-over-65).

### If you have a history of an eating disorder

Appetite-suppressing medicines can be harmful for people with restrictive eating disorders. A responsible service will screen for this and may decline. If this applies to you, speaking to your GP first is the safest route.

### If you are planning a pregnancy

GLP-1 medicines should not be used in pregnancy, and the SmPCs advise stopping in advance of a planned pregnancy. Look for a service that asks about this and explains contraception, especially for Mounjaro, which can reduce the effectiveness of the oral contraceptive pill for a period after starting and after each dose increase.

## Subscriptions, cancellations and pausing treatment

Many online pharmacies supply treatment on a repeating basis. Before you start, it is worth understanding:

- Whether you are signing up to a subscription, and how often you will be charged
- How to pause treatment, for example before surgery or if you become unwell
- How to cancel, and whether there is a notice period
- What happens to any medicine already dispatched if you cancel
- How you will be told about price changes

Clear, fair terms are a sign of a well-run service. Be cautious about any arrangement that makes it hard to stop, or that commits you to paying upfront for a long period.

## Putting it together: a checklist

| Check | What good looks like |
|---|---|
| GPhC (or PSNI) registration | Pharmacy found on the register; details match the website |
| Named people | Superintendent pharmacist and prescribers named and registered |
| Care regulator | Any online doctor service registered (for example with the CQC in England) |
| Assessment | Detailed medical questionnaire, not just BMI |
| Verification | Identity checked; weight independently verified |
| GP involvement | Consent sought to share information with your GP |
| Medicines | Licensed products only, at licensed doses |
| Aftercare | Reviews before dose increases; easy access to clinicians |
| Delivery | Cold-chain packaging; clear guidance on storage and sharps |
| Costs | Clear about total costs, inclusions and how prices change |
| Marketing | No urgency, pressure or promotional claims about medicines |

## Online pharmacy, online doctor or clinic?

Some people prefer a fully online service, while others value face-to-face contact. There are trade-offs in convenience, continuity and how verification works. Our guide to [weight loss clinics vs online providers](/guides/weight-loss-clinics-vs-online-providers) compares the models, and our guide to [online doctor weight loss services](/guides/online-doctor-weight-loss-uk) explains how online prescribing works.

The NHS is another route for people who meet NICE criteria, although access remains limited and is being phased in. See our guide to [private vs NHS weight loss treatment](/guides/private-vs-nhs-weight-loss-treatment).

## Switching pharmacy

If you are thinking of changing pharmacy mid-treatment, plan it carefully. The new prescriber will need to reassess you, verify your weight and identity, and usually see evidence of your previous prescription. Avoid long gaps, which may mean restarting at a lower dose, and never hold prescriptions from two services at once. Our guide to [switching providers mid-treatment](/guides/switching-providers-mid-treatment) explains how.

## When to speak to a professional

Talk to your GP or a community pharmacist if you are unsure whether a GLP-1 medicine is right for you, if you have other health conditions or take regular medicines, or if you are unsure whether an online service is legitimate. A pharmacist can help you check registration and talk through options. Prescribing decisions always belong to a qualified prescriber.

If you are taking a GLP-1 medicine:

- **Call 999 or go to A&E** if you have severe, persistent abdominal pain (with or without vomiting, sometimes spreading to your back), signs of a severe allergic reaction such as swelling of the face, lips or throat or difficulty breathing, or you collapse.
- **Contact NHS 111 or your GP urgently** if you have persistent vomiting or diarrhoea with signs of dehydration, or pain under the right ribs with fever or yellow skin or eyes.
- **Stop using the product and seek advice** if you think you have received a fake or tampered pen, and report it to the MHRA through the Yellow Card scheme.
