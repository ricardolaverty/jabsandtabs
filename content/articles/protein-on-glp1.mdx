If you are taking Mounjaro (tirzepatide), Wegovy (semaglutide) or another GLP-1 medicine, protein deserves more attention than it would on an ordinary diet. These medicines reduce appetite so effectively that many people simply eat less of everything, including protein. Too little protein during rapid weight loss can mean losing more muscle than necessary. A widely cited 2025 joint advisory suggested that many adults losing weight on GLP-1 treatment may benefit from roughly 1.2 to 1.6 g of protein per kilogram of body weight a day, spread across meals, alongside strength exercise. This guide explains what that means in practice.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Some of the weight lost on any weight-loss treatment is lean tissue, not just fat. In trial body-composition substudies, lean mass made up roughly a quarter to two-fifths of total weight lost.
- A 2025 joint advisory from four US societies suggested around 1.2 to 1.6 g of protein per kg of body weight a day during weight reduction, often about 80 to 120 g a day in practice.
- Spreading protein across three or more small meals tends to be easier on a slow-emptying stomach than one large protein-heavy meal.
- Lean, simply cooked protein (fish, eggs, yoghurt, poultry, tofu, pulses) is usually better tolerated than fatty, fried or rich dishes.
- Protein is most effective alongside regular strength exercise. Diet alone cannot fully protect muscle.
- People with kidney disease, diabetes, eating disorders or other medical conditions should get individual advice from a GP or registered dietitian before changing protein intake.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Why protein matters on GLP-1 treatment

### Weight loss is never only fat

Whenever someone loses a significant amount of weight, whether through diet, surgery or medication, the body loses a mixture of fat and lean tissue. Lean tissue includes skeletal muscle, but also organs, water and other non-fat tissue. That is normal and, to some extent, expected: a smaller body needs less supporting tissue.

The question is the balance. Researchers have measured body composition in GLP-1 trials using DXA scans (a low-dose X-ray that estimates fat and lean mass):

- In a substudy of the **SURMOUNT-1** trial of tirzepatide, roughly three-quarters of the weight lost was fat and about one-quarter was lean mass over 72 weeks.
- In a DXA substudy of the **STEP 1** trial of semaglutide 2.4 mg, lean mass accounted for a larger share, close to two-fifths of total weight lost, although the proportion of the body made up of lean tissue still rose because fat fell faster.

These proportions are broadly in line with what is seen with other forms of calorie restriction. They do not mean GLP-1 medicines "destroy muscle". They do mean it makes sense to take practical steps to protect muscle, especially for older adults and anyone who already has low muscle mass. Our guide to [preserving muscle on GLP-1 treatment](/guides/preserving-muscle-on-glp1) goes into the evidence in more depth.

### Appetite falls, and protein falls with it

GLP-1 medicines act on appetite centres in the brain and slow the rate at which the stomach empties. The result is that people feel full sooner and stay full for longer. That is the point of the treatment, but it creates a nutritional challenge: if you are eating perhaps half of what you used to, the quality of each mouthful matters more.

People often find that the foods they still fancy on treatment are lighter, starchier or sweeter, such as toast, crackers or fruit, while meat, fish and larger meals feel less appealing. Over weeks and months, this can quietly push protein intake well below what the body needs.

### Protein also supports other things

Beyond muscle, adequate protein supports wound healing, immune function, and the maintenance of hair, skin and nails. Some people notice hair shedding during rapid weight loss (see our guide to [Mounjaro and hair loss](/guides/mounjaro-hair-loss)). This is usually linked to the stress of weight loss itself, but a diet that is very low in protein and other nutrients is unlikely to help.

## How much protein might you need?

### UK reference values

In the UK, the reference nutrient intake for protein for most adults is about 0.75 g per kilogram of body weight per day. That figure was set for the general healthy population, not specifically for people losing weight rapidly.

### The 2025 joint advisory

In 2025, the American College of Lifestyle Medicine, the American Society for Nutrition, the Obesity Medicine Association and The Obesity Society published a joint advisory on nutritional priorities for people taking GLP-1 medicines for obesity. On protein, it said:

- Higher intakes, around **1.2 to 1.6 g per kg per day**, have been proposed during weight reduction to help preserve lean mass.
- In practice, this often works out at around **80 to 120 g a day**.
- Intakes should not routinely exceed about **2 g per kg per day**.
- There is genuine uncertainty about whether these targets should be based on actual body weight, an "adjusted" body weight, or lean mass, especially for people with a high BMI.

That last point matters. For someone weighing 130 kg, 1.6 g per kg of actual weight would be over 200 g a day, which is impractical and probably unnecessary. This is one reason a dietitian or clinician may use a target based on a lower reference weight.

<Callout type="evidence">
The joint advisory is a consensus document from US professional societies, not a UK clinical guideline. NICE guidance on overweight and obesity management (NG246) emphasises a balanced, healthy diet and physical activity alongside medication but does not set a specific protein target for GLP-1 users. Treat the figures above as a sensible starting point for discussion with your care team, not a prescription.
</Callout>

### Rough daily targets

The table below shows what different targets look like at a range of body weights. It is illustrative only.

| Body weight used for calculation | 0.75 g/kg (UK reference) | 1.2 g/kg | 1.6 g/kg |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 kg | 45 g | 72 g | 96 g |
| 75 kg | 56 g | 90 g | 120 g |
| 90 kg | 68 g | 108 g | 144 g |
| 105 kg | 79 g | 126 g | 168 g |

If these numbers look high compared with what you are currently managing, you are not alone. Many people on GLP-1 treatment start well below their target. The aim is steady improvement, not perfection.

### Who should get individual advice first

Please speak to your GP, prescriber or a registered dietitian before deliberately increasing protein if you:

- have chronic kidney disease or have been told your kidney function is reduced
- have type 1 or type 2 diabetes, particularly with kidney involvement
- have liver disease
- have, or have had, an eating disorder
- are pregnant, breastfeeding or planning a pregnancy (GLP-1 medicines for weight loss are not used in pregnancy)
- are over 75 or frail, where needs can be different and appetite is often already low
- have a history of gout or kidney stones

## Practical ways to eat enough protein with a small appetite

### Put protein first

A simple habit that many dietitians suggest is to eat the protein part of a meal first. If you fill up after a few mouthfuls, which is common on GLP-1 treatment, you will at least have eaten the most important part. Vegetables and starchy foods still matter for fibre and energy, but they can follow.

### Spread it across the day

Rather than relying on one large evening meal, aim for a source of protein at every meal and most snacks. Three small meals plus one or two protein-rich snacks is a pattern many people find workable. Smaller, more frequent meals are also one of the strategies the 2025 advisory suggested for managing nausea and fullness.

### Choose forms that are easy to tolerate

Because these medicines slow stomach emptying, fatty and fried foods can sit heavily and may worsen nausea, burping or reflux. You will often do better with:

- poached, grilled, baked or steamed fish and poultry
- eggs (boiled, scrambled or in an omelette)
- dairy foods such as Greek-style yoghurt, skyr, cottage cheese and milk
- tofu, tempeh and edamame
- well-cooked lentils, beans and chickpeas, in modest portions
- soft textures such as soups, stews and casseroles, which some people find easier when appetite is low

For more on which foods commonly cause trouble, see our guide to [foods that can worsen GLP-1 side effects](/guides/foods-that-worsen-glp1-side-effects).

### Liquid and soft options for difficult days

Some days, especially in the first few days after an injection or after a dose increase, solid food can feel like hard work. Options that may be easier include:

- milk, or milk-based drinks
- yoghurt drinks or kefir
- smooth soups made with lentils, beans or blended chicken
- scrambled eggs
- smoothies made with yoghurt or milk and soft fruit

### What about protein shakes and bars?

Protein shakes and bars are not essential, but they can help some people who struggle to meet their needs from food. If you use them:

- read the label: some contain a lot of added sugar, others contain sweeteners or sugar alcohols that can cause wind, bloating or loose stools
- treat them as a top-up, not a replacement for meals, because whole foods also provide fibre, vitamins and minerals
- sip slowly rather than drinking quickly, which may be easier on the stomach
- be cautious with products marketed with weight-loss or "GLP-1" claims, which are food supplements and are not regulated as medicines

## Protein and fibre: getting the balance right

Constipation is common on GLP-1 treatment (see our guide to [constipation on GLP-1 medication](/guides/constipation-on-glp1)). One risk of focusing heavily on protein is crowding out fibre, which comes from vegetables, fruit, wholegrains, nuts, seeds and pulses.

Pulses such as lentils, beans and chickpeas are useful here because they provide both protein and fibre. Pairing protein with vegetables or a wholegrain at most meals, and keeping fluids up, helps to cover both bases. If you find high-fibre foods worsen bloating early on, increase them gradually.

## Protein and exercise: why both matter

Protein provides the raw material, but muscle needs a reason to stay. Resistance exercise, sometimes called strength training, sends that signal. The 2025 joint advisory recommended regular strength training at least three times a week plus at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity, while the UK Chief Medical Officers' guidelines recommend muscle-strengthening activities on at least two days a week for all adults.

You do not need a gym. Bodyweight exercises, resistance bands and simple home routines can all help. Our guides to [exercise on GLP-1 medication](/guides/exercise-on-glp1) and [strength training for beginners](/guides/strength-training-for-beginners-on-glp1) give step-by-step options.

## Common protein myths on weight loss injections

### "More is always better"

Not true. Beyond a certain point, extra protein does not build more muscle, especially without exercise, and very high intakes can displace other important foods. For people with kidney disease, high intakes may be harmful. The joint advisory suggested not routinely exceeding around 2 g per kg per day.

### "You can only absorb 30 g at a time"

This is an oversimplification. The body digests and uses protein from larger meals too. However, on GLP-1 treatment the practical limit is usually appetite, not absorption, so spreading protein across the day is still sensible.

### "Plant protein does not count"

Plant proteins contribute fully to daily intake. Some individual plant foods contain lower amounts of certain amino acids, but eating a variety of plant sources across the day covers this. Soya, in particular, is a high-quality protein.

### "Protein stops nausea"

There is no reliable evidence that protein prevents nausea. Some people find a little plain protein, such as a boiled egg or yoghurt, settles their stomach better than sugary snacks, but others find protein-heavy foods harder to face when queasy. Go with what you can tolerate.

## Special situations

### If you have type 2 diabetes

GLP-1 medicines are also used in type 2 diabetes, and people taking insulin or sulphonylureas alongside them can be at greater risk of low blood sugar, according to the product information. Changing your diet significantly may alter your glucose levels. Work with your diabetes team before making major changes, and see our guide to [GLP-1 medicines and type 2 diabetes](/guides/glp1-and-type-2-diabetes).

### If you are older

Muscle mass naturally declines with age, and older adults often need at least as much protein as younger people, if not more, to maintain it. At the same time, appetite is often lower. Protein and strength exercise are particularly important for older adults on GLP-1 treatment, and our guide to [GLP-1 medicines over 65](/guides/glp1-over-65) covers this in more detail.

### If you have a history of disordered eating

Tracking protein grams can be helpful for some people and harmful for others. If counting, weighing or monitoring food has been difficult for you in the past, please talk to your GP or a specialist dietitian about an approach that feels safe.

## When to speak to a professional

Contact your GP, prescriber or pharmacist if:

- you are regularly eating very little (for example, only a few mouthfuls at each meal) for more than a few days
- you are losing weight much faster than expected, or feel weak, faint or unusually tired
- you notice significant hair loss, brittle nails or slow wound healing
- you have kidney disease, diabetes or another long-term condition and want to change your diet
- you have a history of an eating disorder and find food tracking is affecting your wellbeing

Seek urgent help (call NHS 111, or 999 in an emergency) if you have:

- severe, persistent abdominal pain, with or without vomiting, which can be a sign of pancreatitis or gallbladder problems
- signs of dehydration that do not improve, such as passing very little dark urine, dizziness or confusion
- vomiting that prevents you keeping down any fluids

Decisions about your medication, including whether to continue, change dose or stop, belong to your prescriber. Nutrition advice in this guide is general and does not replace individual assessment by a registered dietitian or doctor.
