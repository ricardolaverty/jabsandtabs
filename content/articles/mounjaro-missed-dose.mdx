If you miss a Mounjaro (tirzepatide) injection, the UK product information gives a simple rule: if no more than four days (96 hours) have passed since the dose was due, inject it as soon as possible and then go back to your usual weekly day. If more than four days have passed, skip the missed dose and take the next one on your normal day. Never double up to make up for a missed dose. You can also change your injection day if you need to, as long as doses are at least three days (72 hours) apart. After longer gaps of two weeks or more, speak to your prescriber before restarting, as you may need a lower dose.

This guide explains the rule, works through common scenarios, and covers what to do after longer breaks, accidental extra doses and missed doses caused by illness, travel or supply problems. For more on how dosing works overall, see [Mounjaro doses explained](/guides/mounjaro-dosing-schedule) or visit the [Mounjaro hub](/mounjaro).

## The rule from the UK product information

The Mounjaro Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC) and patient information leaflet set out what to do if a dose is missed:

- **If four days or fewer have passed** since the missed dose was due, take it as soon as possible. Then take your next dose on your usual day.
- **If more than four days have passed**, skip the missed dose. Take your next dose on your usual day.

In either case, you then resume your normal once-weekly schedule.

### Why four days?

The four-day window ensures that there are always at least three days between two doses. Tirzepatide has a half-life of about five days, so taking two doses very close together would push blood levels higher than intended and make side effects such as nausea and vomiting more likely. The rule balances getting your treatment back on track with avoiding doses that are bunched too closely. See [How Mounjaro works](/guides/how-does-mounjaro-work) for more on why it lasts a week.

## Worked examples

These examples assume your usual injection day is Monday.

| When you remember | Days since dose was due | What the rule says | Next dose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tuesday | 1 day | Inject now | Following Monday |
| Wednesday | 2 days | Inject now | Following Monday |
| Thursday | 3 days | Inject now | Following Monday |
| Friday (within 96 hours) | 4 days | Inject now | Following Monday |
| Saturday | 5 days | Skip this dose | Following Monday (two days away) |
| Sunday | 6 days | Skip this dose | Following Monday (the next day) |

If your Monday injection is usually in the evening and you remember on Friday afternoon, you are within the 96 hours. If you remember on Friday night, you may be just past it. If you are unsure whether you are within the window, it is safer to ask your pharmacist or prescriber than to guess.

## Changing your injection day

Sometimes it suits you better to move your injection day permanently, for example after a missed dose or because of a change in work pattern. The SmPC allows this as long as the time between two doses is at least three days (72 hours).

| Current day | New day you want | Is it allowed? |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Thursday (3 days later) | Yes: at least 72 hours between doses |
| Monday | Wednesday (2 days later) | No: less than 72 hours; wait until Thursday or later, or move to the following Wednesday |
| Monday | Saturday (5 days later) | Yes |
| Monday | The following Tuesday (8 days later) | Yes: this is effectively a slightly late dose |

After you change, inject weekly on the new day. Setting a phone reminder for the new day can help.

## What a missed dose means for your treatment

### Appetite and side effects

Many people notice their appetite increasing slightly if a dose is late or skipped, as tirzepatide levels fall. Some also find that side effects such as nausea briefly return when they take their next dose, particularly after a longer gap.

### Weight

A single missed or late dose is unlikely to make a meaningful difference to your overall progress. Regularly missing doses is a different matter: in trials, consistent weekly dosing produced the results reported, and the SURMOUNT-4 trial showed that stopping treatment altogether led to substantial regain. If you are struggling to remember doses, it is worth finding a system that works for you and discussing any barriers with your prescriber. See [Mounjaro results over time](/guides/mounjaro-results-timeline).

### Dose escalation

If you miss a dose while on a step-up schedule, your prescriber may advise staying on the same strength for a little longer before increasing, so that your body has had a full four weeks at that dose. Do not move up to the next strength without advice.

## Longer gaps: two weeks or more

The SmPC does not provide a detailed plan for restarting after several missed doses. In practice, prescribers use clinical judgement, considering:

- how long you have been without treatment
- which dose you were on
- how well you tolerated it before
- whether you have had any illness, surgery or new medicines during the gap

| Length of gap | What prescribers commonly consider |
|---|---|
| One dose skipped (under two weeks without a dose) | Usually resume your usual dose on your usual day |
| Around two to three weeks | Contact your prescriber; resuming the same or a lower dose may be considered |
| Four weeks or more | Contact your prescriber; restarting at a lower dose and stepping up again is often considered |

*These are illustrations of common clinical considerations, not instructions. Your prescriber will advise on your situation.*

The main reason for caution is that tolerance to the digestive effects of tirzepatide falls during a break. Restarting straight at a high dose, such as 10mg or 15mg, after several weeks without treatment could cause marked nausea, vomiting or diarrhoea, with a risk of dehydration. See [Mounjaro side effects](/guides/mounjaro-side-effects-guide).

## Accidental extra doses

Mistakes can happen, for example injecting a dose and then forgetting you had done so, or taking a missed dose too close to your next one.

If you think you have taken too much, or two doses less than three days apart:

- **contact your prescriber, pharmacist or NHS 111** for advice
- **watch for stronger side effects**, such as nausea, vomiting and abdominal discomfort
- **stay hydrated** and eat small, plain meals if you can
- **if you take insulin or a sulfonylurea**, check your blood sugar more often and follow your hypoglycaemia plan

Do not skip your next dose or change your schedule without advice. If you develop severe abdominal pain, persistent vomiting or signs of dehydration, seek urgent help.

## Why consistent weekly dosing matters

Tirzepatide is designed to keep fairly steady levels in your body with one injection every seven days. With a half-life of about five days, levels fall gradually between injections and rise again after each dose. A dose taken a day or two late slightly lengthens the low point; a skipped dose lets levels fall further before the next injection.

In practical terms:

- **A short delay** usually has little effect, although some people notice more hunger.
- **A skipped dose** may lead to a noticeable return of appetite for a few days and sometimes a brief return of side effects with the next injection.
- **Several missed doses** allow levels to fall low enough that the body partly loses its tolerance to digestive side effects, which is why prescribers often restart at a lower dose.

Keeping to a regular weekly routine is the simplest way to get the benefit that was seen in trials.

## Tools that can help you stay on schedule

| Tool | How it helps |
|---|---|
| Recurring phone alarm | Prompts you on the same day and time each week |
| Calendar entry with the pen strength | Reminds you of the day and which dose you are on |
| Written or app log | Records each injection so you can check whether a dose was taken |
| Marking the pen carton | Tracks how many of the four doses have been used |
| Linking to a weekly habit | Ties the injection to something you already do every week |

## Common reasons for missed doses

### Illness

If you are unwell with vomiting, diarrhoea or a high temperature, or you cannot eat and drink normally, contact your prescriber or pharmacist before your next injection. Taking Mounjaro while dehydrated can increase the risk of kidney problems. They may advise delaying a dose until you have recovered. If the delay goes beyond four days, the skip rule applies.

### Travel

Changing time zones can make it easy to lose track of your injection day. Keeping your injection time roughly consistent with your home time, or moving the day using the 72-hour rule, can help. Carry your pen in hand luggage and keep it within the recommended storage temperatures. See [storing and travelling with Mounjaro](/guides/mounjaro-storage-and-travel).

### Surgery and procedures

You may be advised to pause Mounjaro before surgery or procedures under sedation or general anaesthetic, because it slows stomach emptying and increases the risk of aspiration. This is a planned interruption rather than a missed dose. Follow the advice of your surgical team and prescriber about when to stop and restart. See [GLP-1 medication before surgery and anaesthesia](/guides/glp1-surgery-and-anaesthesia).

### Supply and delivery problems

Delivery delays or stock problems can interrupt treatment. If you think your next pen will not arrive in time:

- contact your provider or pharmacy as early as possible
- do not try to stretch a pen by spacing out doses or taking partial doses
- do not obtain Mounjaro from unregulated sources, which risk fake or unsafe products

If you are moving to a new provider, plan the transfer so there is no gap, and share your current dose and the date of your last injection. See [Switching providers mid-treatment](/guides/switching-providers-mid-treatment) and [Fake and unlicensed weight loss jabs](/guides/fake-weight-loss-jabs).

### Forgetting

Simple strategies can help:

- link your injection to a weekly routine, such as a particular evening
- set a recurring phone alarm or calendar reminder
- keep a written or app-based log of each injection and which pen dose you used
- store the pen somewhere you will see it, within storage guidelines

## A quick missed-dose checklist

When you realise a dose has been missed, working through a few questions can help:

1. **When was the dose due?** Work out the day and approximate time.
2. **How long has passed?** Four days (96 hours) or less: take it now. More than four days: skip it.
3. **When is my next dose due?** Make sure there will be at least three days (72 hours) between doses.
4. **Am I well?** If you are vomiting, have diarrhoea or cannot keep fluids down, get advice before injecting.
5. **Have I missed more than one dose?** If you have been without treatment for two weeks or more, contact your prescriber first.
6. **Was I due a dose increase?** Ask your prescriber whether to stay on your current strength for a little longer.

## What not to do after a missed dose

- **Do not take two doses together** or on consecutive days to catch up.
- **Do not move up to a higher strength** to compensate.
- **Do not use leftover medicine** from a finished pen to make up a dose.
- **Do not restart at a high dose after a long break** without speaking to your prescriber.
- **Do not obtain pens from unregulated sources** if your supply has run out.

## More scenarios

**You remember on the day your next dose is due.** If a whole week has passed, the missed dose is more than four days late, so skip it and take your usual dose that day. Do not take two.

**You injected but are not sure the full dose went in.** Do not inject again. Contact your pharmacist or prescriber for advice, and take your next dose on your usual day unless told otherwise.

**You are travelling across time zones.** Aim to keep doses roughly seven days apart. If you need to shift your day, make sure there are at least 72 hours between doses, and set reminders in your home time zone if that helps.

**You are waiting for a delivery.** Contact your provider early. If your dose ends up more than four days late, skip it and restart on your usual day once your pen arrives, and ask for advice if the gap becomes longer.

## Missed doses and contraception

Missing a Mounjaro dose does not, on its own, affect hormonal contraception. However:

- the SmPC advises considering a non-oral method, or adding a barrier method, for four weeks after starting Mounjaro and for four weeks after each dose increase, which may also be relevant if you restart after a break or at a different dose
- vomiting or severe diarrhoea can make the pill less reliable, so follow your pill's guidance on illness

Mounjaro should not be used during pregnancy. If you think you may be pregnant, stop taking it and contact your prescriber. See [Mounjaro, GLP-1s and contraception](/guides/glp1-and-contraception).

## Missed doses and type 2 diabetes

If you take Mounjaro for weight management and also have type 2 diabetes, a missed dose may cause your blood sugar to rise. Check your levels as advised by your diabetes team. When you restart, especially after a longer break, your other diabetes medicines may need reviewing, particularly insulin or sulfonylureas, which increase the risk of low blood sugar. See [GLP-1 medication and type 2 diabetes](/guides/glp1-and-type-2-diabetes).

## How providers handle missed doses

A good provider, NHS or private, should make it easy to get advice about missed doses and to adjust your plan. When comparing services, it can be useful to check:

- how quickly you can reach a pharmacist or prescriber
- whether they have a clear process for re-titration after a break
- how they handle delivery delays and urgent supply problems
- whether subscriptions can be paused or adjusted

## When to speak to a professional

Contact your prescriber, pharmacist or GP:

- if you are unsure whether you are within the four-day window
- if you have missed two or more doses in a row, or been without treatment for two weeks or more
- before restarting after any break, particularly at a dose of 7.5mg or higher
- if you think you have taken an extra dose
- if you are unwell, vomiting or unable to keep fluids down when your dose is due
- if you have type 2 diabetes and your blood sugar has changed

**Seek urgent medical help (NHS 111, or 999 in an emergency) if you have:**

- severe, persistent abdominal pain, which may spread to your back, with or without vomiting
- vomiting that will not stop, or signs of severe dehydration such as very little urine, dizziness or confusion
- symptoms of severe low blood sugar, such as confusion, drowsiness or fitting, especially if you take insulin or a sulfonylurea
- swelling of the face, lips or throat, or difficulty breathing
