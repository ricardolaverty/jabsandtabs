Weight loss clinics and online providers can both offer safe, regulated access to GLP-1 medicines such as Mounjaro (tirzepatide) and Wegovy (semaglutide). The main difference is how you are assessed and supported. A clinic sees you face to face, so a clinician can weigh and examine you directly. An online provider assesses you remotely, verifies your details by video call, GP records or other methods, and posts your medicine from a registered pharmacy. Neither model is automatically safer. What matters is registration, the quality of the assessment, the source of the medicine, and the support you receive. This guide compares the options so you can decide, with your prescriber, what suits you. For more, see our [provider guides](/guides/topic/providers) and our full library of [GLP-1 guides](/guides).

<KeyTakeaways>
- "Weight loss clinic" can mean an NHS specialist service, a private medical clinic, a pharmacy clinic or, sometimes, an unregulated aesthetics or beauty setting. Check exactly what you are dealing with.
- Clinics offer in-person measurement and examination; online providers offer convenience and often faster access.
- Both must use registered prescribers and registered pharmacies, and clinical services carrying out regulated activities must register with the relevant care regulator, such as the CQC in England.
- Since February 2025, GPhC guidance has said online prescribing of weight-loss medicines should not rely on a questionnaire alone.
- People with complex medical needs may benefit from face-to-face care or close GP involvement.
- Compare total costs over time, not just the starting price. We explain what affects cost on our prices page.

## What counts as a "weight loss clinic"?

The phrase covers a wide range of services, and the differences matter.

| Type | Who runs it | How GLP-1 medicines are accessed | Regulation |
|---|---|---|---|
| NHS specialist weight management service (tier 3) | NHS multidisciplinary team | Semaglutide under NICE TA875; other treatments per local pathways | NHS; CQC (England) or equivalent |
| NHS primary care | GP practice | Tirzepatide being phased in under NICE TA1026 for people meeting priority criteria | NHS; CQC (England) or equivalent |
| Private medical weight loss clinic | Doctors, nurses, dietitians | Private prescription after in-person assessment | CQC (England) or equivalent; prescribers' regulators |
| Community pharmacy clinic | Pharmacist prescribers | Private prescription after in-pharmacy assessment | GPhC (Great Britain) or PSNI (Northern Ireland) |
| Aesthetics or beauty clinic | Varies | Must involve a registered prescriber and a pharmacy to be legal | Varies; some operate outside the rules |

### A note on aesthetics and beauty settings

Some aesthetics clinics employ registered prescribers and work properly with registered pharmacies. Others have offered "skinny jabs" without a genuine assessment, sometimes using products from unregulated sources. The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) has warned repeatedly about unlicensed weight-loss products, and in October 2025 reported dismantling an illicit production facility for unlicensed tirzepatide and retatrutide pens. If you are offered weight loss injections in a beauty or aesthetics setting, check who the prescriber is, their registration, and which registered pharmacy supplies the medicine. Our guide to [fake and unlicensed weight loss jabs](/guides/fake-weight-loss-jabs) explains the warning signs.

## What counts as an "online provider"?

Online providers usually fall into two groups: online pharmacies that employ their own prescribers, and online doctor services that work with a partner pharmacy. Some also offer coaching, apps or dietitian support. Our guide to [online doctor weight loss services in the UK](/guides/online-doctor-weight-loss-uk) explains these models in detail.

| Feature | Face-to-face clinic | Online provider |

| Convenience | Requires travel and appointments | Assessment from home; delivery |
| Speed of access | Varies; NHS services often have waiting lists | Often quicker |
| Continuity | Often the same clinician or team | May see different clinicians |
| Behavioural support | Some clinics include dietitian, psychology or group support | Varies; some include apps or coaching |

## The case for face-to-face care

### Direct measurement and examination

A clinician can weigh you accurately, measure your waist and blood pressure, and examine you if something seems wrong. This removes the verification challenges of remote services.

### Better for complex needs

If you have several long-term conditions, take many medicines, have type 2 diabetes treated with insulin or sulfonylureas, have kidney disease, or have a history of eating disorders, an in-person assessment, or an online service working closely with your GP, may be more appropriate. Our guide to [who should not take GLP-1 medicines](/guides/who-should-not-take-glp1s) explains the conditions that need extra care.

### Multidisciplinary support

NHS specialist weight management services and some private clinics offer access to dietitians, psychologists, physiotherapists and doctors. NICE guidance on overweight and obesity management emphasises that medicines should be used alongside a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity, and multidisciplinary support can help with this.

### Relationship and continuity

Seeing the same clinician over time can make it easier to discuss sensitive issues, spot changes and adjust treatment.

## The case for online providers

### Convenience

You can be assessed without taking time off work or travelling, which matters for people with busy schedules, caring responsibilities, mobility issues or who live far from a clinic.

### Access and speed

NHS access to GLP-1 medicines is limited by NICE criteria and phased rollout. Online providers can often assess people sooner, within the medicine's licence.

### Structured follow-up

Many online services build in regular reviews, reminders and messaging with clinicians.

### Discretion

Some people feel more comfortable discussing weight from home.

## What to expect at a face-to-face clinic appointment

If you choose an in-person clinic, the first appointment usually takes longer than an online assessment, because more can be measured and discussed in the room. A typical first visit may include:

- **Measurements:** weight, height, waist circumference and blood pressure, taken by the clinician.
- **Medical history:** the same core questions as an online service, covering weight-related conditions, previous pancreatitis or gallbladder problems, digestive conditions, eating disorders, mental health, pregnancy plans and contraception, and all your medicines.
- **Possible tests:** some private clinics offer, or require, blood tests such as HbA1c (a measure of average blood sugar), kidney function and cholesterol. NHS services may already have recent results.
- **Discussion of options:** which medicine might suit you, how it is started and increased, what side effects to expect, and how progress will be judged.
- **Injection training:** a clinician may show you how to use the pen, and sometimes supervise the first injection.
- **Lifestyle planning:** in clinics with dietitians or other professionals, you may get a plan for eating, protein intake and physical activity.

Follow-up visits are usually shorter and focus on weight change, side effects and whether to increase the dose. Some clinics combine in-person visits with phone or video reviews between appointments.

## What to expect from an online provider

An online provider usually follows a sequence that looks like this:

1. You complete a detailed medical questionnaire.
2. Your identity is checked electronically or with photo ID.
3. Your weight is verified independently, for example by a video consultation, a check of your GP record, or a measurement taken at a pharmacy.
4. A registered prescriber reviews the information, asks follow-up questions if needed, and decides whether treatment is appropriate.
5. A registered pharmacy dispenses the medicine and posts it in cool packaging.
6. You are reviewed before dose increases, typically by questionnaire, message or video, with updated weight.

Our guide to [the online weight loss consultation](/guides/online-weight-loss-consultation) explains the questions in detail, and our guide to [online doctor weight loss services](/guides/online-doctor-weight-loss-uk) explains the different online models.

## Assessment quality: the most important factor

Whatever the setting, a safe assessment should cover:

- Identity and age
- Accurate, verified weight and height (BMI)
- Weight-related conditions
- Medical history, including pancreatitis, gallbladder disease, digestive problems, kidney and liver disease, diabetic eye disease, and thyroid cancer history
- Eating disorder and mental health screening
- Pregnancy, breastfeeding and contraception
- All current medicines
- Previous GLP-1 use and doses

For online providers, GPhC guidance published in February 2025 says prescribing weight-loss medicines should not be based solely on an online questionnaire, and weight, height or BMI should be independently verified. Our guides to [the online weight loss consultation](/guides/online-weight-loss-consultation) and [why online pharmacies verify your ID and weight](/guides/id-and-weight-verification) explain this.

## Regulation: what to check in each case

### For a clinic

- Search the CQC website (England) or the equivalent regulator (HIW, HIS or RQIA) for the clinic, and read any inspection report.
- Check the prescriber on the GMC, GPhC or NMC register.
- Ask which registered pharmacy supplies the medicine, and check it on the GPhC (or PSNI) register.

### For an online provider

- Check the dispensing pharmacy on the GPhC (or PSNI) register, using the details on its website.
- Check any separate online clinical service on the CQC website (England) or equivalent.
- Check named prescribers on their professional registers.

Our guide to [who regulates online weight loss services](/guides/who-regulates-online-weight-loss-services) explains how these regulators fit together.

## Medicines and evidence: the same in both settings

The medicine and the evidence behind it are the same whichever route you use. In the SURMOUNT-1 trial, tirzepatide 15mg produced average weight loss of around 21% over 72 weeks. In STEP 1, semaglutide 2.4mg produced average weight loss of around 15% over 68 weeks. In both trials, participants also received lifestyle advice. Individual results vary, and no service can promise a particular outcome. Our guide to [realistic weight loss expectations](/guides/realistic-weight-loss-expectations) explains what the averages mean.

What can differ is the support around the medicine: how carefully doses are increased, how side effects are managed, and whether you get help with diet, protein intake and physical activity to protect muscle. See our guides to [what to eat on GLP-1 medicines](/guides/what-to-eat-on-glp1) and [preserving muscle on GLP-1 treatment](/guides/preserving-muscle-on-glp1).

## Lifestyle support matters in every setting

GLP-1 medicines are licensed for use alongside a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity, and the major trials included lifestyle counselling for all participants. NICE guidance on overweight and obesity management (NG246) also places medicines within a wider approach that includes diet, activity and behavioural support.

This matters for a few reasons:

- **Nutrition.** Eating much less can make it harder to get enough protein, fibre, vitamins and minerals. A dietitian or well-designed programme can help.
- **Muscle.** Some of the weight lost on GLP-1 medicines is lean mass, including muscle. Resistance exercise and adequate protein intake can help protect it. See our guide to [strength training for beginners on GLP-1 medicines](/guides/strength-training-for-beginners-on-glp1).
- **Long-term habits.** The STEP 4 and SURMOUNT-4 trials found that people who stopped treatment regained a substantial proportion of the weight they had lost. Habits built during treatment may help if you later reduce or stop medication, although they do not fully prevent regain.

Some clinics and online providers include dietitian, coaching or app-based support; others focus mainly on prescribing. Neither model is inherently better at this, so it is worth asking what is offered and whether it suits you. Our guide to [protein on GLP-1 medicines](/guides/protein-on-glp1) is a useful starting point.

## Cost considerations

NHS treatment is free at the point of use, although prescription charges apply in England for people who are not exempt. Private clinics and online providers are self-funded, and costs vary. Factors that affect what you pay include:

- The medicine and dose (higher doses often cost more)
- Consultation and review fees
- Whether needles, sharps bins and delivery are included
- Additional services such as dietitian support or blood tests
- Subscription terms
- Changes in list prices, such as Lilly's increase to the UK list price of Mounjaro from September 2025

Face-to-face clinics may cost more due to premises and staff time, but this is not always the case, and the total depends on what is included. We don't quote prices in our guides. You can compare current costs on our [prices page](/prices), and our guide to [weight loss treatment hidden costs](/guides/weight-loss-treatment-hidden-costs) explains what to look out for.

## Hybrid options

The choice isn't always either/or. Some people:

- Are assessed online but have their weight and blood pressure measured at a local pharmacy
- Start treatment through an online provider while their GP monitors their other conditions
- Use an online provider for medication and a separate dietitian or exercise professional for support
- Start privately while waiting for NHS specialist services

Whatever the arrangement, keep your GP informed so your medical record is accurate.

## Moving between settings

People sometimes start in one setting and move to another, for example starting privately while waiting for NHS specialist care, or moving from a clinic to an online provider for convenience. Whatever the direction, a few principles apply:

- **Bring your history.** Your new prescriber will want to know which medicine you have taken, your current dose, the date of your last injection, your starting weight and any side effects. Keep copies of prescriptions and dispensing labels.
- **Expect to be reassessed.** Each new prescriber is responsible for their own decision, so they will verify your identity and weight and review your medical history.
- **Avoid gaps.** A long break can mean restarting at a lower dose to reduce side effects.
- **Never hold two prescriptions at once.** This is unsafe and may mean you receive more medicine than intended.
- **Keep your GP informed** so your record stays accurate.

Moving from private to NHS care is not automatic. NHS treatment depends on meeting NICE criteria and local availability, and private treatment history does not by itself make you eligible for NHS treatment. Our guide to [switching providers mid-treatment](/guides/switching-providers-mid-treatment) covers this in more detail.

## Questions to ask a clinic or online provider

Whichever model you are considering, these questions can help you understand what is on offer:

- Who will assess and prescribe for me, and what is their registration?
- Is the service registered with the CQC (or equivalent in Wales, Scotland or Northern Ireland)?
- Which registered pharmacy supplies the medicine?
- How will my weight and identity be checked, and how often?
- Will you contact my GP?
- How often will I be reviewed, and in what format?
- How do I get advice quickly if I have side effects?
- What lifestyle or dietary support is included?
- What happens if treatment isn't working, or if I want to stop?
- What is included in the cost, and how are price changes communicated?

## Which might suit you?

| If you… | You might consider… |
|---|---|
| Have complex health needs or many medicines | Face-to-face care, or an online service with close GP involvement |
| Value convenience and have straightforward health needs | An online provider with robust verification |

| Meet NICE criteria | Ask your GP about NHS options first |
| Prefer seeing the same clinician | A clinic or service that offers continuity |
| Have a history of eating disorders | Speak to your GP first; specialist assessment is likely to be more appropriate |

## Warning signs in any setting

- No registered prescriber, or one you cannot find on a register
- No genuine medical assessment
- Injections given on the spot from an unlabelled pen or vial
- Products described as "research", "compounded" or with unfamiliar names
- Pressure to sign up, pay upfront for long periods or "act now"
- Promises of specific weight loss or before-and-after images used to promote medicines

## When to speak to a professional

Talk to your GP before choosing a clinic or online provider if you have other medical conditions, take regular medicines, are planning a pregnancy, or have a history of eating disorders, pancreatitis or gallbladder disease. Your GP can also tell you whether you might be eligible for NHS treatment. Prescribing decisions always belong to a qualified prescriber.

If you are taking a GLP-1 medicine:

- **Call 999 or go to A&E** if you have severe, persistent abdominal pain (with or without vomiting, sometimes spreading to your back), signs of a severe allergic reaction such as swelling of the face, lips or throat or difficulty breathing, or you collapse.
- **Contact NHS 111 or your GP urgently** if you have persistent vomiting or diarrhoea with signs of dehydration, or pain under the right ribs with fever or yellowing of the skin or eyes.
- **Seek advice and report to the MHRA's Yellow Card scheme** if you think a product you received is fake, unlabelled or tampered with.
