There are no foods that are strictly forbidden on Mounjaro (tirzepatide), Wegovy (semaglutide) or other GLP-1 medicines. But many people find that certain foods and eating habits make the common digestive side effects, such as nausea, bloating, burping, reflux and diarrhoea, noticeably worse. The usual culprits are fatty, fried and very rich foods, large portions, eating quickly, very sugary foods, fizzy drinks and alcohol. This guide explains why, what the evidence says, and gentler alternatives to try while your body adjusts.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Digestive side effects are very common on GLP-1 medicines. Nausea, diarrhoea and constipation are listed as very common in the Mounjaro and Wegovy product information.
- These medicines slow stomach emptying, so foods that also slow digestion, especially fatty and fried foods, are a frequent trigger.
- Portion size and pace often matter as much as the food itself. Stopping at the first sign of fullness is one of the most useful habits.
- Fizzy drinks, alcohol, very spicy food and sugar alcohols in "sugar-free" products are other common triggers for some people.
- Side effects usually peak when starting treatment and after dose increases. Many people can reintroduce foods later in smaller portions.
- Persistent vomiting, severe abdominal pain or signs of dehydration need medical advice, not just diet changes.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Why food choices affect GLP-1 side effects

### Slower stomach emptying

GLP-1 medicines mimic natural gut hormones that are released after eating. One of their effects is to slow the rate at which food passes from the stomach into the small intestine. This helps people feel full for longer, which is part of how these medicines support weight loss.

The downside is that food stays in the stomach longer than you are used to. If you eat the same portion you would have eaten before treatment, or a meal that is naturally slow to digest, your stomach can feel overfull. That can lead to nausea, a heavy or bloated feeling, burping, heartburn and, sometimes, vomiting.

### How common are these side effects?

Digestive side effects are the most frequently reported problems in GLP-1 trials:

- In the **SURMOUNT-1** trial of tirzepatide for weight management, nausea was reported by roughly a quarter to a third of participants depending on dose, compared with under one in ten on placebo. Diarrhoea and constipation were also common.
- The **Wegovy** product information, based on pooled trial data for semaglutide 2.4 mg, lists nausea in about 44% of people, diarrhoea in about 30% and constipation in about 24%.

In both cases, side effects were mostly mild to moderate and most frequent during the dose-escalation period. That is why diet adjustments tend to matter most in the early weeks and around each dose increase. For a detailed look at these symptoms, see our guides to [nausea on GLP-1 medication](/guides/nausea-on-glp1) and [diarrhoea on GLP-1 medication](/guides/diarrhoea-on-glp1).

<Callout type="info">
Food changes can ease symptoms, but they do not replace medical advice. If side effects are severe or are stopping you eating and drinking, your prescriber may consider slowing your dose increases or other changes. That decision belongs to them, so let them know.
</Callout>

## Foods and drinks that commonly cause problems

### Fatty, fried and greasy foods

This is the most consistent trigger reported by people on GLP-1 treatment, and it makes physiological sense: fat itself slows stomach emptying. The 2025 joint advisory from four US obesity and nutrition societies suggested avoiding fatty foods, particularly in the first few days of treatment, to help reduce nausea and other gastrointestinal effects.

Common examples include:

- takeaway foods such as fried chicken, chips, kebabs, battered fish and pizza with heavy toppings
- full English breakfasts with fried bread, sausages and bacon
- creamy sauces, cheesy pasta bakes and rich curries made with lots of ghee, butter or cream
- pastries, sausage rolls, pies and croissants
- crisps and other deep-fried snacks

Some people tolerate small amounts of these foods; others find even a few chips cause hours of discomfort.

### Large portions

You may find you are full after a much smaller portion than before. Continuing to eat past that point, out of habit, politeness or a desire not to waste food, is one of the most common reasons for nausea and vomiting on these medicines. The advisory specifically mentions avoiding large meals for people at risk of vomiting.

Practical tips:

- serve meals on a smaller plate
- start with half your usual portion and go back for more only if you are genuinely hungry
- save leftovers for later rather than finishing everything
- pause halfway through a meal and check how full you feel

### Eating too quickly

Fullness signals take time to register. Eating quickly makes it easy to overshoot, especially when stomach emptying is slowed. Putting your cutlery down between mouthfuls, chewing thoroughly and taking 20 minutes or so over a meal can make a real difference.

### Very sugary foods and drinks

Sweets, cakes, biscuits, sugary cereals and sweet drinks can be easy to eat when appetite is low, because they go down without much effort. However, some people report that very sweet foods make them feel sick on GLP-1 treatment, and large amounts of sugar in one go can contribute to loose stools. They also provide energy with few nutrients, which matters when total intake is reduced.

### Fizzy drinks

Carbonated drinks, including sparkling water, add gas to the stomach. For many people on GLP-1 medicines, this increases bloating, burping and the feeling of fullness. Some find a few sips of something fizzy settles nausea, so this is individual, but if you are bloated it is worth trying still drinks for a week or two.

### Alcohol

Alcohol can irritate the stomach lining, relax the valve at the top of the stomach (making reflux more likely), and worsen nausea. The 2025 joint advisory stated that alcohol may worsen nausea and gastro-oesophageal reflux with GLP-1 therapy and should be minimised. For people with diabetes on insulin or sulphonylureas, alcohol can also increase the risk of low blood sugar.

If you choose to drink, the UK Chief Medical Officers' guidelines advise not regularly drinking more than 14 units a week, spread over three or more days. Many people find their tolerance and desire for alcohol changes on GLP-1 treatment. Our guide to [Mounjaro and alcohol](/guides/mounjaro-and-alcohol) covers this in more detail.

### Very spicy foods

Chilli and other strong spices can trigger heartburn and indigestion in some people, and this may be more noticeable when food sits in the stomach for longer. You do not necessarily need to give up flavour: herbs, garlic, ginger, lemon and milder spices are often well tolerated.

### High-fibre foods, introduced too quickly

Fibre is important, especially for preventing constipation, but a sudden large increase in beans, lentils, bran, raw vegetables or wholegrains can cause wind, bloating and cramping. The advisory suggested that very high-fibre foods may be worth limiting in the first few days of treatment, then increased gradually. This is a balancing act: you still want enough fibre overall. Our guide to [constipation on GLP-1 medication](/guides/constipation-on-glp1) explains how to build fibre up steadily.

### Sugar alcohols and some sweeteners

Sugar alcohols such as sorbitol, xylitol, maltitol and erythritol are found in many sugar-free sweets, chewing gum, protein bars and "diet" products. In larger amounts they can cause wind, bloating and diarrhoea in anyone, and the effect may be more noticeable when your gut is already sensitive.

### Foods that commonly trigger reflux

If heartburn or acid reflux is your main issue, the NHS lists common triggers that may be worth limiting, including fatty and spicy foods, alcohol, coffee, chocolate and tomato-based foods, alongside lifestyle factors such as eating late at night. Our guide to [reflux, burping and indigestion on GLP-1 medication](/guides/reflux-burping-and-indigestion-on-glp1) goes into this in depth.

## Eating habits that matter as much as food choice

### Stop at the first sign of fullness

This is probably the single most useful habit. On GLP-1 medicines the signal of fullness can arrive suddenly. If you notice it, stop, even if there is food left. Eating "just a few more bites" is a common cause of feeling unwell an hour later.

### Eat little and often

Many people find three small meals with one or two light snacks easier than two or three large meals. The joint advisory described an approach of a small breakfast followed by small meals every three to four hours with adequate fluids.

### Drink between meals rather than with them

Large drinks with meals can add to the feeling of fullness. Try sipping fluids steadily through the day and taking smaller amounts with food. Staying hydrated is still important, especially if you have diarrhoea or vomiting. See our guide to [hydration on GLP-1 medication](/guides/hydration-on-glp1).

### Do not lie down straight after eating

Staying upright for two to three hours after a meal can reduce reflux. If night-time heartburn is a problem, raising the head of the bed slightly may help.

### Notice your patterns

Side effects often vary over the week, depending on when you inject. Some people notice symptoms are worse in the first day or two after their injection. Planning lighter meals on those days, and keeping richer meals for later in the week, can help. A simple food and symptom diary for a couple of weeks can help you spot your own triggers.

## What about specific foods people ask about?

### Dairy

Most people tolerate dairy well, and lower-fat dairy foods such as yoghurt, skyr, milk and cottage cheese are useful sources of protein. Full-fat cream, rich cheese and ice cream may be harder for some people. If you have known lactose intolerance, lactose-free alternatives are widely available.

### Red and processed meat

Lean red meat in moderate portions is usually fine. Fatty cuts, sausages, burgers and processed meats are more likely to cause trouble because of their fat content. UK guidance already advises that people who eat more than 90 g of red and processed meat a day cut down to 70 g or less, regardless of medication.

### Coffee and caffeine

Coffee does not interact with GLP-1 medicines, but it can trigger reflux in some people and may worsen loose stools. Caffeine is not a substitute for fluids if you are struggling to drink. If coffee seems to make things worse, try reducing it or switching to decaffeinated.

### Bread, pasta and rice

These are rarely a problem in modest portions, although very large servings can cause fullness. Wholegrain versions provide more fibre. Plain starchy foods such as toast or crackers are often well tolerated when nausea is at its worst.

### Chocolate

Chocolate combines fat and sugar, and it is a recognised reflux trigger for some people. A small amount is unlikely to cause harm, but larger quantities, especially of milk chocolate, may worsen symptoms.

## Foods that people often find easier

Everyone is different, but foods that are commonly well tolerated, particularly in the early weeks, include:

- plain toast, crackers, rice cakes and plain rice
- porridge or overnight oats in small portions
- eggs, boiled or scrambled without lots of butter
- lean chicken, turkey or white fish, baked or poached
- plain or Greek-style yoghurt
- soups and broths
- cooked vegetables such as carrots, courgettes and green beans
- bananas, apples, pears and berries
- ginger tea or peppermint tea

For a fuller picture of balanced eating on treatment, see our guide to [what to eat on GLP-1 medication](/guides/what-to-eat-on-glp1) and our [sample week of meals](/guides/glp1-meal-plan). Protein is particularly important, as explained in our guide to [protein intake on weight loss injections](/guides/protein-on-glp1).

## A note on oral GLP-1 medicines

Tablet forms of GLP-1 medicines can have specific instructions about food and drink. For example, oral semaglutide is usually taken on an empty stomach with a small amount of water, and food must be delayed for a set period afterwards. The details differ between products and licences, so always follow the patient information leaflet and your prescriber's instructions. ## Eating out, takeaways and social occasions

Restaurant meals, takeaways and celebrations are where many people run into trouble, because portions are larger, food tends to be richer, and there can be social pressure to finish your plate. A few strategies can help:

- **Look at the menu in advance.** Many restaurants publish menus online, which makes it easier to spot grilled, baked or tomato-based options without feeling rushed.
- **Order a starter as a main**, or share a main course. Most people on GLP-1 treatment find a full restaurant portion far too much.
- **Ask for sauces and dressings on the side**, so you can control how much you have.
- **Choose one richer element rather than several.** For example, if you want chips, pair them with grilled fish rather than battered fish and mushy peas cooked in butter.
- **Go easy on alcohol and fizzy drinks**, or alternate them with still water.
- **Take leftovers home.** It is a practical way to avoid overeating without feeling you have wasted food.
- **Have a short, honest phrase ready** if people comment on how little you are eating, such as "I'm full, but it was lovely". You do not owe anyone details about your health or medication.

For takeaways, lighter choices often include grilled chicken or fish dishes, tandoori or tikka dishes without creamy sauces, stir-fried or steamed dishes with plain rice, sushi or sashimi, and salads with lean protein. Battered, deep-fried and creamy dishes are the ones most often reported as causing problems.

If you know a big meal is coming, for example a wedding or a holiday, it may help to keep earlier meals that day small and light, and to avoid timing the event right after an injection or a dose increase where possible. Never change your injection day or dose without checking with your prescriber first, though, because the timing of doses is part of your treatment plan.

## Is it the food, or something else?

Not every symptom on GLP-1 treatment is caused by food. Side effects can also be linked to:

- starting treatment or a recent dose increase
- dehydration
- constipation, which can itself cause nausea and loss of appetite
- other medicines or illnesses
- rarely, more serious problems such as gallbladder disease or pancreatitis, which are listed in the product information

If you have made sensible diet changes and are still struggling, it is worth talking to your prescriber. Some providers offer more support than others with managing side effects, which you can explore on our [provider comparison page](/providers).

## When to speak to a professional

Speak to your GP, prescriber or pharmacist if:

- nausea, bloating or reflux are stopping you eating enough or affecting daily life
- you are vomiting regularly, even if you can keep fluids down
- diarrhoea lasts more than a few days
- you have lost your appetite almost entirely for several days
- you have diabetes and your blood sugar readings are unusually low or high
- you have a history of an eating disorder and find food avoidance is becoming a worry

Get urgent help (call NHS 111, or 999 in an emergency) if you have:

- severe abdominal pain that does not go away, particularly if it spreads to your back, with or without vomiting, as this can be a sign of pancreatitis
- pain in the upper right side of your tummy, fever or yellowing of the skin or eyes, which can indicate gallbladder problems
- vomiting that means you cannot keep down any fluids
- signs of dehydration, such as passing little or no urine, dizziness, confusion or a racing heart
- vomit containing blood, or black, tarry stools

Any decision to change your dose, pause or stop treatment should be made with your prescriber.
